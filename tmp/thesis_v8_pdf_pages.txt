Abstract
Reinforcement Learning for an Orbital Target Defense Game
with Navigation and Control Constraints
Gus Santaella, MSE
The University of Texas at Austin, 2026
SUPERVISOR: Maruthi Akella
This thesis focuses on the use of reinforcement learning for adversarial planning
within the framework of realistic guidance, navigation, and controls for orbital space-
craft. With this in mind, this thesis proposes the Phased Adversarial Reinforcement
Learning plus Behavior Cloning (PARL+BC, for short) framework to train robust
policies in a two-player, zero-sum Target-Attacker-Defender (TAD) orbital defense
scenario. To test this, we study a two-player zero-sum orbital adversarial planning,
extending the framework to partial observability of an opponent’s state through be-
havior cloning with bearings-only Kalman-filter state estimates, studying how learned
policies interact with a maximum velocity limiting classicalCBF-QP safety controller
in-the-loop, and evaluating if policies trained underHCW dynamics can transfer to
previously unseen elliptical linear time-varying dynamics. The Monte Carlo results
show that our proposed framework produces coherent and competitive defender and
attacker policies for a zero-sum orbital defense game.

Chapter 1: Introduction
Adversarial planning against non-cooperative agents has become a relevant
area of research in real-world autonomous systems and robotics problems. Adversarial
problems arise when an autonomous agent must make decisions while accounting
for the actions of another intelligent and non-cooperative opponent with opposing
objectives. These sorts of problems appear in various multi-agent planning settings,
such as pursuit-evasion, target-attacker-defender (TAD) asset defense, and robotic
guidance tasks. Examples of these sorts of games are provided in the literature review
in chapter 3. In any of these, a successful outcome for an agent depends not only
on their actions and the system dynamics, but also on the evolving strategy of its
opponents. Traditional optimal control frameworks to solve these kinds of problems
tend to assume certain information strategies which can be jeopardized against an
agent that’s following a shifting, actively adversarial strategy coming from a versatile,
intelligent opponent.
In these cases, classical control architectures may prove to be brittle against
these highly dynamic strategies. Namely, this can happen when the opponent doesn’t
follow an assumed strategy, information is limited, or if solving for a pursuit-evasion
game through online optimization methods becomes computationally demanding.
When coupled with estimation and safety constraints in the loop, these difficulties can
become even more pronounced.
Inspired by this dilemma, we chose to develop a reinforcement-learning (RL)
based approach for planning against these sorts of adversarial opponents. Instead of
solving an optimization game online on every timestep, theRL framework concentrates
mostofitsonlinecomputationalburdenontraining, thereforeproducingadeterministic
policy with fast inference speeds during rollout. The benefit behind theseRL-based
architectures is that, when properly trained, these can produce a single, deterministic
18

===== PDF PAGE 19 =====
policy that can actively react and adapt to a wide range of scenarios it’s been trained
against.
One of the arguments behind why research and industry have not widely
adopted RL algorithms within their autonomy/GNC work comes down to their lack
of formal guarantees for stability and convergence. This lack of guarantees can be
attributed to the fact that theseRL-based algorithms try to optimize against highly
non-convex objectives while using gradients from noisy trajectories, approximate the
value functions they learn during training, are prone to overfit to the data distribution
that they’re trained on, and struggle to interpolate to data outside this distribution.
In other words, these factors can lead to a noisy stochastic gradient ascent/descent
process that finds locally optimal solutions instead of the globally optimal solution
that may not be guaranteed to work in regimes outside the training dataset. In fact,
the development of betterRL algorithms that can account for these mishaps present
in the current state-of-the-artRL is a core area of research in world of theoretical
deep RL in this day and age.
Regardless, algorithms such as Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO, Schulman
et al. (2017)), an on-policy algorithm created by OpenAI, remain one of the most
widely used algorithms inRL research for engineering applications (François-Lavet
et al. (2018)). In simple terms,PPO is based on the principles of stochastic gradient
ascent on a clipped surrogate objective function to be optimized. The benefits of these
PPO-based architectures are that they’re relatively simple to set up, they reuse each
rollout batch on multiple gradient steps, are easy to parallelize, and, most importantly,
provide much more stability than vanilla policy gradient methods. For-controls based
engineering applications, which is what we’ll be studying in this work, perhaps the
biggest benefit ofPPO is that it can be used for both continuous and discrete action
spaces.
With all of the above in mind, we set off to study a reinforcement-learning
framework to train, model, and rollout robust adversarial policies for orbital zero-
19

===== PDF PAGE 20 =====
sum games in Low Earth Orbit (LEO). In order to better understand how these
architectures could interact with classical autonomy methods, we were most interested
in studying how this framework could learn while subject to a closed-loop aerospace
guidance, navigation, and control architecture (GNC). For our own applications, we
wanted to solve a few questions in this research:
• Can anRL architecture usingPPO learn coherent policies for continuous control
of orbital spacecraft in LEO in zero-sum adversarial scenarios?
• Can anRL architecture learn coherent policies while interacting subject to the
constraints of a classical controller in the loop?
• Can an RL architecture learn coherent policies if, instead of having access
to the opponent’s full state, it had partial observability and had to rely on
measurements in the loop?
• Given boundaries imposed by the controls and estimation architectures, can an
RL architecture maintain coherent performance on observations outside of the
training dataset even if it was never trained against some of that data?
• Can anRL architecture do all of the above at once, and still provide convergent
and high-performing policies despite the stochasticity?
• Can anRL architecture trained withHCW linear time-invariant orbital relative
dynamics transfer to elliptical linear time-varying dynamics that were not seen
during training?
With this in mind, we propose the Phased Adversarial Reinforcement Learning
and Behavior Cloning (PARL+BC) method for training and rollout ofRL-enabled
Guidance, Navigation, and Control (GNC) aerospace applications. The overarching
goal of thePARL+BC framework, as a whole, is to develop a phasedPPO-based
learning framework that makes it possible to applyRL formulations in a dynamic
20

===== PDF PAGE 21 =====
adversarial problem with physical constraints. Our method uses dense step per
geometry-based rewards and phased opponent freezing to mitigate non-stationarity
encountered in multi-agent training and aid learning stability.PARL+BClearns while
subject to the constraints of a classical velocity-saturatingCBF-QP controller and,
optionally, partial observability of the opponent’s state via a bearings-only Extended
Kalman Filter (EKF), which is achieved by using behavior cloning.
Our Monte Carlo evaluation results show thatPARL+BC produces highly
performing policies in a constrained, zero-sum orbital asset defense game with equal
spacecraft characteristics. The defender policies manage an aggregate success rate
of up to 79.9% across four learning phases in the fully observable setting, where
the opponent is also guided by anRL-trained policy. For the Extended Kalman
Filter-based policies, where neither agent has direct access to the opponent’s state
and instead has to estimate it through a bearings-onlyEKF, the defender succeeds in
up to 74.7% of the cases, a 6.51% relative drop in performance relative to the fully
observable policies. We performed all our evaluations across bothHCW and elliptic
dynamics, even though the policies were never exposed to the latter during training,
from which we found at most a 1.29% relative change between results across dynamics
models. Furthermore, these results were achieved while providing fast rollout and
compute inference times of 0.145 ms median per timestep on the full-state policies
and 3.91 ms per timestep on the EKF-based policies.
Our findings open the way for the development of highly computationally
efficient RL-based methods for autonomous systems research and application, and a
discussion of potential directions for future work is provided.

Chapter 2: Problem Formulation
To test our hypothesis for thePARL+BCframework, we propose a constrained
zero-sum game, based on linear-time-invariant 2nd-orderHCW dynamics (or linear-
time-variant elliptic dynamics) to model the interaction of two agents relative to a
chief circular orbit in the Local-Vertical Local-Horizontal (LVLH) frame. We bound
these agents into an imaginary spherical arena in space centered at the chief of the
orbit. In our initial setup, we have two agents and a static element in the environment:
1. Asset/Object of Interest (OI): A static object in theLVLHframe that is
the "chief" of the orbit.
2. Player 1 (defender):Defends an asset of interest by preventing player 2 from
intercepting the asset of interest by capturing it. If it intercepts player 2 or
forces it out of the spherical arena, it wins.
3. Player 2 (attacker):Attacks the central chief asset of interest by preventing
player 1’s defense strategy. If it intercepts the asset of interest, forces the
defender to crash into it, or forces the defender out of the spherical arena, it
wins.
The result is a variation of a pursuit-evasion scenario where the conditions
above make it a zero-sum, spatially constrained game that simplifies the learning
problem for an onlineRL algorithm such asPPO. Figure 2.1 offers a visual depiction
of the proposed game.
The states are the positions and velocities of each agent and the actions are
continuous control inputs in 3D space, where we assume both spacecraft to be fully
actuated in three translational degrees of freedom. For the sake of simplicity, we
ignore the attitude evolution in this work, and we assume that Player 1 and Player 2
are simply looking at each other at all times.

2.3 Full-State and Partial Observability Settings
Note that this work also studies the proposed framework under two information

agent has to estimate its opponent’s state via a bearings-only Extended Kalman Filter

to assess how the proposed framework degrades when moving toward more realistic
sensor-based navigation settings.

2.4 Assumptions
Considering the problem that has been set up throughout this chapter, we will
make a few assumptions throughout the remainder of this manuscript:
• Both spacecraft agents are fully actuated and have continuous throttle control
on each translational axis.
• The spacecraft used by both agents in the game are identical and have the same
control parameters.
• Both agents follow the same dynamics model relative to the chief of the orbit.
• Attitude evolution is ignored throughout for the sake of simplicity: only transla-
tional motion is considered and in theEKF setting the agents are considered to
be facing each other at all times.
• The raw control actions from the learned policies are assumed to be realizable
subject to the CBF-QP saturation filter.
• There is no actuator lag or delay across either spacecraft: the only lag present is
the discrete ∆t timestep in the simulation.
• In the full-state setting of the game, each agent has access to its opponent’s full
state.
• Also in the full-state setting of the game, the orbital game is treated as a Markov
Decision Process (MDP) by both agents.
• In the partial-observability setting of the game, the opponent state is only
obtained through bearings-only measurements.
• The defender and attacker remain within a bounded spherical arena during
training and evaluation and, if they leave it, rollouts stop.

initial-condition dispersions are assumed to be adequately represented by the
parameters chosen in the simulation.
• External environmental effects, such as atmospheric drag and solar wind, are

Chapter 3: Literature review
As we mentioned before, this work focuses on a two-player zero-sum orbital
Target-Attacker-Defender game in Low Earth Orbit (LEO), the research problem is
more difficult than a standard single-agent guidance problem. This is because an
agent’s best action depends not only on the orbital dynamics and arena geometry, but
also on the changing behavior of an intelligent adversary. As our framework accounts
for both full-state and partial-state information cases, the learned policies must operate
while interacting with a classicalGNC architecture, including theCBF-QP controller
and, only in the partially observable setting, a bearings-onlyEKF to estimate the
opponent’s state. With this problem setup in mind, the most relevant prior literature
includes orbital pursuit-evasion games, differential games, reinforcement learning for
adversarial planning, and estimation and control-constrained learning methods.
Orbital pursuit-evasion and adversarial guidance games have become a prevalent
area of research in aerospace autonomy academia. Plenty of examples of related work
have been published in recent years, mostly on optimal-control-based methods:
Gong et al. (2020) developed a classical differential game of satellite pursuit-
evasion under continuous thrust using a Clohessy–Wiltshire (CW) model. Moreover,
Selvakumar and Bakolas (2017) developed a two-player zero-sum differential game
with input constraints, providing a semi-analytical capture-time solution to their
problem using closed-form optimal controls. Then Liu et al. (2025) proposed a
one-step look-ahead Nash equilibrium method for cooperative pursuit-evasion in
cooperative spacecraft. And lastly, Drucker and Shaferman (2026) framed a low-thrust
spacecraft pursuit-evasion problem as a linear-quadratic zero-sum game with terminal
speed constraints, from which they obtain guidance laws via cost-weight selection
demonstrating improved performance against actively evading targets.
In addition to more classical approaches to these rigorous space-bound prob-
29

===== PDF PAGE 30 =====
lems, there has been a rise in journal-published research trying to solve variations of
these games but opting for online reinforcement learning (RL) approaches in recent
years. Despite the longstanding criticism for core reinforcement learning algorithms
to not be able to provide theoretical convergence guarantees to learned policies, re-
searchers have found problems in which have been useful and have provided empirically
convergent policies, strong performance relative to their assigned tasks, and excellent
computational efficiency.
Li and Wang (2025) successfully developed a PPO framework for multi-agent
orbital pursuit-evasion, outperforming shooting methods in their optimality index
metrics in computational efficiency while providing robustness to suboptimal opponent
strategies. Next, Cao et al. (2026) proposed the HPRR-PPO algorithm to do alternat-
ing learning for non-fixed-duration turn-based orbital game problems, showing effective
interception against evaders with compute speed suitable for real-time deployment
and reported a compute runtime of 1 ms per made decision. Furthermore Zhang et al.
(2022) used deep reinforcement learning to achieve near-optimal guidance laws in one
to one orbital pursuit-evasion games. Finally, Zhao et al. (2023) proposed a MADDPG
based method for impulsive orbital pursuit-evasion.
The fact that all these papers above have been published in the past few years
makes it clear that there is growing interest in developing reinforcement learning-based
methods for planning in adversarial orbital dynamics. This is especially relevant
given the generalized growing interest within the autonomy community to implement
machine learning-based frameworks to complement or even replace classical control
architectures.
Furthermore, additional work has been made in the area of estimation-awareRL
frameworks. While physical AI is something roboticists and industry alike are pushing
towards, one must remember that real-life robots won’t have full-state measurements
of their environment as we can achieve in a simulation. Instead, they need to use
sensors to characterize their surroundings and plan accordingly. Generally speaking,
30

===== PDF PAGE 31 =====
this is problematic on the decision-making side, as it can turn a clean Markov Decision
Process (MDP) present in a full-state environment into a Partially Observable Markov
Decision Process (POMDP), which is harder to characterize (Kaelbling et al. (1998)).
Ye et al. (2017) showed that modern decision-making under partial observability
can be carried out by planning over an approximated belief tree, showing that near-
optimal policies can be obtained in partial observability without solving for the full
POMDP. Then Igl et al. (2018) showed that reinforcement learning under partial
observability using latent-state inference to do their estimates, meaning that a belief
representation is learned from incomplete observations, showing that this method
outperforms baselines in partially observable tasks. Lastly, Zhang et al. (2015) did
partially observable learning with continuous control, where they handle the problem
by augmenting the policies with continuous memory states, where they end up finding
that learned policies can memorize relevant information needed to act with incomplete
observations.
Moreover, some works have tried to fill in the gaps between learning frameworks
and classical control frameworks by combining the most desirable parts of each into a
single integrated architecture.
Berkenkamp et al. (2017) shows that learning can be constrained via clas-
sical stability/safety filtering, using Lyapunov safety certificates instead of leaving
the learning unconstrained. Koller et al. (2019) bridged safe explorations and rein-
forcement learning through model predictive control, showing that uncertainty-aware
trajectories and terminal safety constraints can be made safely while improving learn-
ing performance. Most importantly for the sake of this work, Emam et al. (2022)
showed how a Control-Barrier-Function (CBF) safety layer can be used to modify the
learned policy’s behavior by projecting the policy actions in a way that induces a safe
learning transitions map, preserving the safety of the learned model while allowing a
reward-based policy to be learned on the side.
We notice a few gaps in the existing literature. Namely, we see that most
31

===== PDF PAGE 32 =====
current orbitalRL work, including those works cited in this literature review, assume
privileged, full-state information within their frameworks, and practically none of the
presented works study learned policies with online estimation in the loop. Almost
none of these papers study the interaction ofRL policies when intertwined with safety
filters and classical control in the loop.
The novelty we present in this work is the integration of these pieces into
a coherent and stablePARL+BC framework. For this, we use a combination of
reinforcement learning techniques that can lead to coherent, robust learning strategies
in zero-sum orbital defense game settings: usingPPO to find learned policies under
the given set of hyperparameters and complex reward shaping, doing so without the
help of an equilibrium/Nash prior solution, and testing how to implement this method
under a partial-observability setting via bearings-only Kalman Filter measurements.
In this work, we contribute to the present literature in the following ways:
• Using a stagedPPO training pipeline instead of residual networks to train the
agents’ policies in a zero-sum, two player orbital game. Namely, the defender and
attacker policies are trained in alternating phases against frozen and randomized
mixtures of opponent policies. The goal of this is to produce policies that become
more specialized, yet more generalizable, to complete their given task within the
game framework instead of overfitting to any one rival.
• Extending upon thePPO defense framework from the previous bullet to partially
observable measurements via behavior cloning. In other words, this work evalu-
ates policies where an agent doesn’t have access to the states of their adversaries.
Instead, these have to be measured via bearings-only Kalman-filter estimates,
based on the work by Aidala (1979). This provides a controlled test on how
learned orbital defense policies degrade under sensor and partial observability
limitations that would be present in a real-world implementation.
32

===== PDF PAGE 33 =====
• Studying the interaction between learned policies in full and partial observability
within the context of a classicalCBF-QP safety filter. This shows how a model-
based classical controller modifies or constrains learning behavior during training
and rollouts.
• Evaluating if policies trained underHCW linear time-invariant relative orbital
dynamics can transfer to elliptical dynamics that were not seen during training.
This should provide a controlled test to see if policies retain coherent behavior
and performance when deployed outside the exact orbital dynamics model used
during training.
So in summary, existing literature shows that orbital pursuit-evasion remains
a relevantGNC problem to research and that reinforcement learning is increasingly
being used by various works to obtain guidance policies. Nevertheless, most of the
existing learning-based work cited above assumes access to privileged state information
and separates the learned guidance problem from a spacecraft’s estimation and safety-
based controls architecture. The contributions made by this work are therefore the
integration of all these algorithms into a phased learning framework.
Specifically, by developing thePARL+BCframework, we research how alternat-
ing, PPO-trained attacker and defender policies perform given opponent randomization,
bearings-only estimator measurements, andCBF-QP safety control filtering. This
positions the work done onPARL+BC as an effective architecture and case study for
work on learning-based orbital defense subject to realistic constraints one would see
in a real-world autonomous vehicle, spacecraft or otherwise.

4.2.1 Partial observability via behavior cloning
One must note that the partially observable policies corresponding to obser-
vations 4.2 and 4.3 also have access this form of a full state reward during training.
This is because on the partially observable policies we want to purposefully match
the measured observations to the full state reward, a process called behavior cloning,
based on the work by Ross et al. (2011).
In this process, we essentially create a measurement-based "student" that must
learn from these fully observable rewards, with the intent of matching the behavior of
the full state rewards as best as possible despite the sensor noise.
This process proves to be perhaps the simplest way to match sensor mea-
surements to RL rewards, which is why it was chosen. There are other potential
methods for training partially observable policies, such as distillation-based methods
38

===== PDF PAGE 39 =====
(e.g., Bajcsy et al. (2024)). We looked into implementing these methods earlier on,
but found them to be too computationally expensive, so we proceeded with regular
behavior cloning in this form.
4.3 Single Phase PPO Algorithm
We collect on-policy results via thePPO algorithm, based on the foundational
work by Schulman et al. (2017) and adapted to our dynamics and environment models.
PPO was selected for this task as it is a proven, practical, and stable on-policy
method for continuous control problems. Compared to other on-policyRL algorithms,
PPO is also relatively straightforward to implement and tune, which proved to be true
when we implemented it within the problem setting for our constrained two-player
orbital training environment. Plus, given its popularity inRL research, there is plenty
of documentation on when and how it can be useful.
The benefit of on-policy algorithms likePPO compared to off-policy or model-
based RL methods is that on-policy algorithms train from purpose-made trajectories
collected during training. On the other hand, off-policy and model-basedRL methods
rely more on previously existing data, which can be brittle if environments are non-
linear, sensitive to mismatches in the training dataset, or if the training data is simply
impossible to obtain.
In the fully observable setting, PPO is applied over the Markov Decision
Process (MDP) induced by the orbital game, where each policy update is built from
state-action-reward transitions collected from training rollout trajectories. In the
partially observable setting, the same overall framework is used, but each agent relies
on the measurements of their opponents as opposed to having access to their full state.

4.3.4 Controller model - CBF-QP velocity saturation controller
One of the later additions to this research was the implementation of aCBF-QP
controller (Ames et al. (2017)) to saturate the velocities of the agents during training
and rollout.
This is consistent with why optimal control problems often put these sorts of
limits as natural constraints on dynamical problems. Without the velocity saturation
limit, the agents would become greedy and myopic with their control output commands.
The result was that the agents, especially the defender, would pick up a lot of speed
by using most of the available control limit, and then struggle to steer and control
themselves during training and rollout.
Therefore, inspired by velocity and control effort saturation which is often
standard in optimization problems, we decided to implement velocity saturation for
both agents into our system, both during training and evaluation. For practical
purposes, this velocity saturation can be justified as a safety barrier, one that bounds
47

===== PDF PAGE 48 =====
the velocities of the agents to avoid unsafe speeds at which they could collide and
cause significant damage to each other, which is not the intent of the proposed game.
One route we considered to implement this was to include a velocity/control
saturating element in the reward function (described in section 4.2), which would have
been possible but we would have lost interpretability of the reward function and the
mean reward return during training by combining various elements with different units
into one monolithic reward function. Generally speaking, as we mention throughout
that section, we found ourRL trainings to behave best the simpler and cleaner our
reward shaping was.
Therefore, we took this as a great opportunity to show how a classical controller
could fill in this gap and do this velocity saturation relative to a predefined max
velocity vmax. We opted to use aCBF-QP, which filters the commanded action relative
to whether it’s considered feasible within the set defined by the velocity constraint. If
the state is safe and the QP is feasible, then the system keeps the overall spacecraft
dynamics within that set. If it is unfeasible, however, the filter will only return
the least-violating admissible action. This would correspond to situations where the
commanded correction to remain within the bounds ofvmax is incompatible with the
available control authority.
The RL-trained policies are directly affected and have to adapt to this new
filter as it becomes a part of the environment the MDP of the PPO algorithm needs
to adjust to. In other words, the policy has to learn from a pre-filtered environment
where unom is not really the applied action. Rather, the safety layer of theCBF-QP
changes the overall transition map used throughout the learning. This, in turn, keeps
the learning setup Markov, at least on the full-state case, as it keeps the applied
control as a deterministic function of the current state and its commanded action.
The massive benefit of this is that theCBF-QP safety filter does not introduce
any hidden dynamics into the controls system or the training logic. Rather, it modifies
the environment learning transition map from one that would learn under raw policy

commandswithoutittoonethatlearnsunderthe CBF-QP-filteredcommands. This, in
turn, ends up providing a computationally efficient constraint on the agents’ velocities
while allowing the training logic to remain as simple as possible by not having to
focus on anything other than the relative distances between the agents and the asset
exclusively. Overall, theCBF-QP was highly performing during training and rollout
right from its implementation, successfully constraining the net agent speed while not
noticeably interfering on the agent’s learning or compute speeds during training or
evaluation.

6.2 Metrics and Evaluation
We evaluate each training policy matchup
(πdef ,0 vs πatt,1, πdef ,1 vs πatt,1, πdef ,1 vs πatt,2, and πdef ,2 vs πatt,2)
by testing them over a fixed time horizonT through Monte Carlo testing. Each
simulation starts from randomized and dispersed initial conditions for both agents
within fixed boundaries for the spherical arena. In each simulation rollout, we report
the following metrics:
Simulation outcome:
The main metric we’re interested in is the outcome of the game: whether the
defender won, whether the attacker won, or if they tie within the limits of the time
horizon. As described in section 4.2, we count a defender win when the defender
captures the attacker or when the attacker leaves the spherical arena. Moreover, we
count an attacker win when the attacker reaches the object of interest (OI), when the
defender crashes accidentally into theOI, and when the defender leaves the spherical
arena. If the maximum time horizon is reached during a simulation, it is counted
as a timeout and a de-facto tie between both agents. For the aggregate number of
rollouts within a Monte Carlo set of runs, we report the number of times in which
each outcome occurred, their corresponding percentage, and the standard error.
Control-effort:
Each simulation also reports the total∆v budget used by each agent over any
given simulation rollout. This gives a measure of the control effort used by each policy
during the simulation. The aggregated ∆v values are then summarized using the
median and interquartile range[Q1, Q3] across all trials within a given Monte Carlo
set.
71

===== PDF PAGE 72 =====
Runtime metrics:
We report the time it took for the policies to compute the rollout per each
time step in the simulation in milliseconds. The objective of this is to keep track
of the real-time computational cost of running the policies in our hardware. The
aggregated values within a given Monte Carlo set are summarized using the median
and interquartile range[Q1, Q3].
Comparison axes.
All of the metrics above are taken for each matchup of
(πdef ,0 vs πatt,1, πdef ,1 vs πatt,1, πdef ,1 vs πatt,2, and πdef ,2 vs πatt,2)
foreachofthetrainingcasesinSection5.1. Allofthesemetricsforeachmatchup
are then run across (i) HCW versus elliptic rollouts, (ii) full-state versus EKF-based
observations via the behavior-cloning framework, and (iii) different sphericalarena
sizes (20 m and 100 m radius). These comparisons allow us to assess the policies’
performance across different dynamics models, robustness to partial observability
and sensor noise, and ability to generalize to different spherical arenas outside of the
training regime.
We do not reportπdef ,0 versus the rule-based attacker throughout this work
because that matchup is intended only as a warm-up phase.

6.3.4.3 Description of Monte Carlo results
We ran a grand total of 57,600 Monte Carlo runs across all matchups within a
single policy test case set, amounting to 172,800 runs across all thesis test cases per
observability setting, and 345,600 runs across both the fully observable and KF-based
evaluations presented in this work. In this wide amount of data, we can note a few
patterns throughout the results presented above. First of all, we can see that in the
20m radius test case evaluations the defender policies become progressively stronger
across the phases, managing to counter newly trained attacker policies throughout
the course of training.
Overall, the aggregate plots tell us a clear story. From the 20mHCW KF off
case, we see that the defender manages to win over the attacker in between 56.3%-
79.5% of the cases in the 20m radius arena that it was trained on, whereas the attacker
wins in only 19.1%-40.7% of the test cases. Tie cases remain minimal at 1.4%-2.9%.
On the 100m radius spherical arena, we note that the results are considerably
different, with the defender winning between 41.0%-66.2% of the cases, and the attacker
winning in 33.4%-58.5% of the cases. Tie cases remain minimal at 0.3%-0.5%, meaning
that the policy matchups were able to reach a termination case in the vast majority
of cases within the provided 10 minute time limit.
Note that the defender was never trained on100 m radius arena observations,
so the drop off is to be expected. If anything, it’s a good result as it signifies a
fairly minimal drop off in performance and tells us that the policies were able to
interpolate to observations outside the training dataset given the symmetry of the
research problem in hand.
For the elliptic results, we see a defender win rate of 56.4%-79.9% and an
attacker win rate of 18.7%-41.1%, with a tie rate of 1.4%-2.5% on the 20 m radius
arena. On the 100m radius arena, the defender win rate is 40.6%-66.1%, the attacker
win rate is 33.5%-59.0%, and tie cases remain at 0.3%-0.4%.

6.4 Analysis of results and anecdotes of development
The results in section 6.3.4.3 show that the proposed PARL+BC method for
adversarial reinforcement learning-based GNC is not only feasible but successful in
constrained zero-sum strategy games with continuous control. We attribute this
finding to the following reasons:
The well-defined, zero-sum objective definition via the reward function and
the episodic termination conditions properly guide agents towards learning
coherent policies
The objectives that both spacecraft have to fulfill are well defined, if not over-
defined, by the reward function plus the episodic terminal conditions. The zero-sum
framing of this problem helps the agents have these defined objectives.
In fact, early on in this research we focused on stand-off games instead of
87

===== PDF PAGE 88 =====
zero-sum games, where the defending agent would have to defend the asset from the
attacker without docking onto the attacker, and both agents would be penalized if
they docked/crashed into each other. This is inherently a non-zero-sum game with
shared equality constraints and an open-loop, standoff objective.
The result was fairly underwhelming after months of research, where we
couldn’t develop successful defender policies. Our conclusion was that you should
try to solve not solve open-loop and undefined learning objectives with reinforcement
learning. Instead, open-loop Nash equilibrium solvers and other classical optimal
control frameworks might best suit these sorts of research tasks.
The constrained volume in space in which the agent is allowed to learn its
policies allows for excellent learning transitions
The learning problem is properly constrained to a volume in space. We don’t
have the agents’ policies search for solutions across infinite or undefined volumes in
space. Instead, by defining arena boundaries, we’re able to constrain the agent’s
learning transitions within a finite area in space.
By training within a confined volume in close-proximity pursuit-evasion, the
agents are forced to train from extremely dynamic behaviors. In the early development
of this work, we tried to train policies exclusively using the larger arena (using a 100
m radius arena during training rather than a 20m radius one) and we found that
this wouldn’t work. Training from a 100m radius arena from the get go would be an
extremely hard task for the agents, especially the defender, and would produce bad
policies .
We suspect that part of this comes from the fact that training from long-
proximity for the problem presented in this work may have to do with the long
transient periods in these arena’s where the agents are not really forced to interact
with each other given much greater amounts of empty space in the arena. Part of
this might be able to be somewhat solved by doing very fine tuning of the learning
88

===== PDF PAGE 89 =====
hyperparameters, be it from more environments, data, exploration, or some other
means. This way agents could properly find solutions within the larger training
environment that match one-to-one with those of the smaller training arena, but it
probably entail much more time and resource intensive.
The trained policies translates partially to larger arenas during evaluation,
even if it was never trained on observations corresponding to larger arenas.
Expanding on the point above, we show that learning in a small, constrained
volume as your environment gives good results, albeit not perfect, in larger volumes
using the same learning policies. This is a rather fascinating find, as we show that
the learned policies don’t simply overfit to the training volume and fail when seeing
observations outside of that volume. We believe that this is in big part thanks to the
symmetry of the research problem proposed in this paper.
Instead of overfitting to the smaller arena’s training data used during training,
the trained policies interpolate agentic behavior learned in the smaller arena into the
larger space, even though it’s never seen the observations corresponding to the larger
space during training.
By training in the smaller arena volume we manage to concentrate and condense
all the agent’s learning transitions into a relatively cheaper training that can then
work in those larger arenas provided the symmetry.
This is not perfect of course, as we did clearly see performance degrade between
the 20m and the 100m arena. Regardless, it’s an exciting result nonetheless.
The velocity saturation controller properly bounds the agent’s behavior
within a "safe" threshold without preventing learning
The CFB-QP velocity saturation controller properly safeguards and bounds
the agents’ actions to a defined limit. In fact, the learned policy clearly learns how to
work within the bounds of the controller and somewhat coexist with it.
89

===== PDF PAGE 90 =====
The development of this finding came from initial testing of learned policies
without the controller on larger arenas. We noticed that agent policies would greedily
pick up a lot of speed and lose control. This meant that the defender would end up
letting the attacker through a lot. After doing a lot of analysis on this phenomenon,
we concluded that the reward function, as presented in section 4.2, does not really
provide an incentive for the agent to safeguard its own actuation for the sake of keeping
controllability within a certain threshold.
Alternatively, one could have put in these incentives into the reward functions,
but we thought that it would have made for a more difficult learning objective as it
would convolute with the terms currently present the reward function. This, in turn,
would have made us lose interpretability of the reward function, meaning the ability
to know what term is causing trouble if learning is good or bad, during policy training
or evaluation.
Instead, we show that following a modular framework like the one we suggest
keeps the guidance learning objectives simple and the velocity controller separate.
This not only, helps remove system-level ambiguities during development, but it proves
our point that classical controllers can coexist with learned modules.
The policies trained in LTI dynamics translate to dynamics with the
provided velocity and acceleration bounds
The results trained under an HCW linear-time-invariant dynamics model hold
within the context of an elliptical linear-time-variant dynamics model with almost
equal performance.
We think that this is due to a couple of things: 1) the fact that the policies
are trained on observations that don’t depend on the dynamics model itself, and
rather just the states of each agent and their adversary, and 2) the HCW or Elliptical
dynamics objectively don’t provide that much environmental acceleration onto the
agents relative to how much actuation limits we’re giving each agent.

6.5 Ideas for future development
For follow ups on the work presented on this thesis, however, I think that there
are a number of areas that could be researched to expand on the scopes of what is
presented here. Some ideas for this include.
• 1) Modifying the reward functions to include rewards for causing poor mea-
surements on opponents, for example by directly incentivizing larger opponent
state-estimation uncertainty under certain sensing and dynamics conditions. This
91

===== PDF PAGE 92 =====
would allow for developing interesting agentic dynamics on the EKF setting.
• 2) Allowing the asset to be able to move along the dynamics model instead of
having it be static at the chief of the orbit. This would allow for policies to be
able to adapt against a moving asset of interest within the dynamics model.
• 3) Adding more elaborate, realistic control models to simulate the actuators on
board the spacecraft with high fidelity. This would allow to further test the
feasibility on this work on more complex estimation and control architectures,
adding up to what you’d see on a real-life spacecraft.
• 4) Testing on more dynamics models (e.g: two-body dynamics, among others) as
well as more challenging orbits, either in training or in evaluation. Although we
showed in this work that our HCW linear-time-invariant training translated nicely
to the elliptical linear-time-variant models, this would help further understand
the feasibility of any given training against environmental adversity from the
dynamics model.
• 5) To test how the PARL+BC framework would work when outputting stochastic
policies instead of deterministic policies, as shown in this work. Although this
could be challenging to achieve convergent policies during trainings, if achieved it
would add yet another layer of uncertainty on the trainings and evaluation that
would allow for even more shifting dynamics and, therefore, the development of
even stronger defender/attacker policies.
It must be noted that the work presented in this thesis is meant to be an
extensive proof of concept of certain ideas that could make RL-based GNC interesting
in theory and simulation. The zero-sum game presented in this work is just meant to
allow us to be a showcase how these RL can be used, whereas there are many other
potential uses for these technologies.

The whole point of this thesis is how one can use RL to derive deterministic
policies for stochastic and unpredictable behaviors within an aerospace context. Even
in planning without adversarial opponents, there are other areas where RL-based
planning would prove to be resourceful, such as:
• 1) To assist in Earth or celestial-bound terrain relative navigation of aircraft and
spacecraft across well-defined start and end points on uncertain terrain. The
work done during the development of PARL+BC showed us that the development
of useful policies is possible as long as the training objectives are closed-loop,
therefore it would be interesting to see how this would translate to other areas
of Earth-bound+space bound GNC.
• 2) To aid in planning on precision spacecraft landings in GPS-denied environ-
ments upon loss of redundancy or function of core navigation sensors (e.g.,
laser-based altimetry). This would allow for onboard GNC policies to be previ-
ously trained against loss of core navigation capabilities in-flight and to be able
to achieve their objectives despite these losses.
• 3) To allow for autonomous orbital relative navigation guidance and navigation
in debris fields that actively shifting or otherwise hard to map. Policies could
be trained to actively avoid orbital hazards while trying to fulfill pre-defined
objectives, just like the defender in this work gets pre-trained to avoid the asset
of interest.
All in all, as long as the principles discussed in section 6.3.4.3 are respected,
regarding the concrete and realistic objectives that shall be given to RL agents for
them to learn appropriate policies, the potential applications for RL to aid or replace
classical GNC architectures are very extensive.

Chapter 7: Conclusion
Throughout this thesis we showed that the proposedPARL+BC method for
RL-based adversarial planning can work within the constraints of a navigation and
control architecture. With the appropriate hyperparameters, the method delivered
convergent policies through phased learning with opponent randomization across a
number of testing scenarios. By doing this, we showed that it is possible to applyRL
formulations in a dynamic adversarial problem with physical constraints.
Our Monte Carlo evaluations of the resulting policies indicate that, in a Target-
Attacker-Defender constrained zero-sum game with 1 defender versus 1 attacker, we
achieved highly performing policies. These results were obtained across numerous
Monte Carlo runs per spherical arena, with 4800 runs on the 20 m radius arena and
9600 runs on the 100 m radius arena. In total, this amounts to 57,600 Monte Carlo
runs across all matchups within a single policy test set, 172,800 runs across all thesis
test cases per observability setting, and 345,600 runs across both the fully observable
and KF-based evaluations presented in this work.
In this evaluation, defender policies succeeded in up to 79.9% in the full-state
policies. Furthermore, defender policies succeeded in up to 74.7% in the partially
observable, EKF-based policies against a competitiveRL attacker, a 6.51% relative
drop in performance relative to the fully observable aggregate four-phase result.
When testing the policies in a larger 100m radius spherical arena, which was
not seen by the policies during training, full-state results achieved up to a 66.2%
defender win rate, a 17.15% relative drop in performance from the 20m radius training
arena, whereasEKF-based policies struggled more with a 47.4% defender win rate, a
28.4% relative drop in performance from the full state case.
Evaluating all of these matchups bothHCW and elliptic dynamics, even though
the policies were never exposed to the latter during training, resulted in at most a
94

===== PDF PAGE 95 =====
1.29% relative change between results in both dynamics models. This shows that the
trained policies can adapt to unmodeled dynamics.
Therefore, across the cases tested in this work, the policies trained under linear
time-invariant dynamics transferred effectively to the linear time-varying dynamics
and with some success to larger arena sizes used during evaluation.
The compute time for these pre-trained policies during inference and rollout
was 0.145 ms median per timestep for the full-state policies and 3.91 ms per timestep
on theEKF-based policies across all documented runs, providing fast compute speeds
that showcase the potential computational advantages of such a method not only in
adversarial space scenarios but in other space autonomy regimes where fast compute
times are required.
These results helped us confirm our initial hypothesis to conclude thatRL
architectures can be used in zero-sum adversarial scenarios, can learn coherent policies
while subject to classical navigation filtering and control in the loop, can remain robust
under full and partial observability, interpolate outside of the training dataset given a
symmetric problem, and provide convergent and highly performing behavior despite
the stochasticity.
Overall, this project showcases that, as long as they’re applied within contexts
with well-defined objectives, deep reinforcement-learning based methods have a feasible
implementation in certain areas of aerospace decision making, without needing to
resort or fallback to classical optimization methods in the process. The fact that
we showed that these results hold under partial observability justifies a potential
hardware application for algorithms along this line of work beyond the theoretical
experiment. This could open the door for further implementations of such aerospace
decision-making algorithms in the future as well as their coupling with existing classical
control architectures.

A.3 Implementation terminology
• Actor: learned residual multi-layer-perceptron (MLP) onϕ with prior blend
βdef ∈ [0, 1], diagonal standard deviationσ = exp(log σ). The log-standard-
deviation terms are learned together with the policy.
• Squash: Mapping of a raw acceleration to a defined actuation limit,ucmd =
umax tanh(uraw), with exact log-Jacobian correction. This lets the policy explore
continuously while respecting the acceleration bound used by the actuators.
• VecEnv: Single-process vectorization. Instead of running one rollout at a
time, we runNenv rollouts in parallel. When one rollout terminates, it is reset
immediately so that the batch keeps the same tensor shape during training.
• Fixed-opponent phase: Training phase where one agent is learning while the
opponent policy is kept fixed. This is used so that the learning problem is less
unstable than if both agents were changing at the same time.
• Estimator-enabled rollout: a rollout where the policy does not observe the
opponent’s true state directly, but instead receives the opponent state estimated
by the EKF. Rewards and terminal conditions are still computed from the true
simulated state.
• Teacher and student policies:the teacher is the policy trained with full-state
information. The student is the estimator-backed policy that is trained to imitate
the teacher when only bearings-only EKF partial observations are available.

Vita
Gus Santaella will graduate in August 2026 with his Masters of Science in Engineering (MSE)
degree in Aerospace Engineering, with an emphasis in Controls, Autonomy, and Robotics and a
graduate certificate in Robotics, at the University of Texas at Austin. He’s been a full-time graduate
research assistant at the Controls Group for Distributed and Uncertain Systems since August 2025,
advised by Dr. Maruthi Akella.
During his master’s program, he completed two internships in guidance, navigation, and
controls engineering. The first was at Blue Origin in Kent, Washington where he worked on the
flight and ground autonomy team testing launch readiness protocols for New Glenn orbital launch
rocket, from January 2025 to May 2025. The second was at Intuitive Machines, where he worked on
the optical navigation team and tested algorithmic improvements to the terrain relative navigation
(TRN) protocols for the Nova-C lander.
Before his graduate degree, Gus earned his Bachelor of Arts in Physics from the University
of Colorado Boulder in May 2022. While there, he worked at JILA/NIST as an experimental physics
research assistant under Eric Cornell and Jun Ye in the JILA eEDM Generation III experiment,
measuring the electron’s electric dipole moment in ThF+ to test the Standard Model of physics,
from September 2020 until February 2023. As part of his involvement in the group, he completed a
thesis on using magnetic coils for Zeeman splitting and for maintaining ambient-field homogeneity of
the ThF+ ions.
After he graduates from his MSE degree, he’ll start full-time employment at Blue Origin in
September 2026, where he’ll work on the guidance, navigation, and controls algorithm team for the
Blue Moon MK1 autonomous lunar lander.
