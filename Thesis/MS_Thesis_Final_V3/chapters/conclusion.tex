\chapter{Conclusion}

Throughout this thesis we showed that the proposed PARL+BC method for RL-based adversarial planning can operate within the constraints of a navigation and control architecture. With appropriate hyperparameters, the method delivered convergent policies through phased learning with opponent randomization across a range of testing scenarios.




Our Monte Carlo evaluations of the resulting policies indicate that, in a Target-Attacker-Defender constrained zero-sum game with 1 defender versus 1 attacker, we achieved highly performing defender policies that succeed in up to 79.9\% in the fully observable case and up to 74.7\% in the partially observable case against a competitive RL attacker, a 6.51\% relative drop in performance from the fully observable aggregate four-phase result. This was done across 4800--9600 Monte Carlo runs per arena.

The compute time for these pre-trained policies during inference and rollout reached 0.145 ms median per timestep for the full-state policies and 3.91 ms per timestep for the EKF-based policies across all documented runs, providing fast compute speeds that showcase the potential computational advantages of such a method not only in adversarial space scenarios but in other space autonomy regimes where fast compute times are required.

These results helped confirm our initial hypothesis: RL architectures can be used in zero-sum adversarial scenarios, can learn coherent policies while subject to classical navigation filtering and control in the loop, can remain robust under full and partial observability, can generalize to some conditions outside the training dataset in a symmetric problem, and can provide convergent and high-performing behavior despite the stochasticity.


Overall, this project shows that, when applied in contexts with well-defined objectives, deep reinforcement-learning methods can provide a feasible approach in certain areas of aerospace decision-making without necessarily falling back to classical optimization methods during execution. The fact that these results continue to hold under partial observability supports the possibility of future hardware-oriented implementations beyond the present theoretical experiment. This could open the door to further aerospace decision-making algorithms of this kind and to their coupling with existing classical control architectures.
